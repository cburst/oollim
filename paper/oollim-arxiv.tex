\begin{abstract}
\textit{oollim} is a locally executable system for evaluating L2 English pronunciation accuracy, fluency, and prosody on a consumer computer. The system combines pretrained XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus representations with reference-text features and ridge regressions trained on SpeechOcean762, the Korean Learners' L2 Speech Corpus (English), J-AESOP, and their combinations. On 305 independently held-out recordings assessed by three expert raters, several ridge configurations substantially outperform archived Azure API scores obtained in January 2026 in both Spearman correlation and quadratic weighted kappa across all three constructs. These results support expert speech evaluation on consumer hardware using pretrained representations and construct-specific regressions, without neural-model fine-tuning or calibration on the private evaluation data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Pronunciation evaluation requires distinct judgments of accuracy, fluency, and prosody. Accuracy concerns the realization of expected consonants and vowels \cite{deterding,cai}; fluency concerns speech continuity, including speed, pauses, and repairs \cite{kormos,bosker}; prosody concerns rhythm, stress, intonation, and phrasing \cite{cole,cutler}. These related dimensions inform pronunciation judgments but are not interchangeable \cite{cai,isaacs}. Segmental accuracy concerns distinctions between speech sounds, whereas intelligibility concerns what listeners understand and comprehensibility their perceived processing difficulty; a noticeable accent need not prevent understanding \cite{munro}. An accuracy score does not directly measure intelligibility. Fluency research distinguishes underlying processing efficiency, observable timing, and perceived smoothness \cite{segalowitz}. Speech rate, pause placement and duration, and repairs contribute differently to listener judgments, so fluency is not simply speaking quickly \cite{kormos,bosker}. Prosody organizes speech through lexical stress, prominence, phrasing, and intonation, with pitch, duration, and intensity providing interacting cues \cite{cole,cutler}. Appropriate stress can improve listeners' recall and evaluations of a speaker \cite{hahn}; contextual appropriateness therefore matters alongside acoustic variation. Although their acoustic correlates overlap, the three constructs require separate targets and useful measurements for educators; the scores need not come from the same representation combination or scoring function. XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus provide complementary sources of speech information. This study examines whether these existing models, combined with modest supervised scoring functions, can support expert L2 English evaluation on an ordinary gaming PC. With computationally expensive pretraining supplied by the original model developers, the contribution is the selection and combination of their representations, reference-text evidence, and corpus-specific assessment supervision. The central evidence is an external evaluation on independently collected, privately held recordings, while training-corpus development results serve model selection and reproducibility and are provided in the repository. The external comparison includes Azure pronunciation assessment \cite{azure}, seven ridge-training families, and two human agreement baselines, with both rank association and numerical agreement measured. This design separates three questions: whether scores rank recordings similarly to human judgments, whether they agree on the numerical scale, and how that agreement compares with agreement among the human raters \cite{williamson}.

\section{Speech models and related work}
Automated assessment has long combined acoustic evidence with supervised mappings to human judgments. SpeechRater derives pronunciation, fluency, and other proficiency features from recognized speech and uses learned scoring models \cite{speechrater}. Neural assessment extends this approach by learning representations and their relationship to rated performance. GOPT uses phonetic posterior features and canonical phone identities in a transformer trained jointly on phone-, word-, and utterance-level human annotations \cite{gopt}. On SpeechOcean762, the 2,500-utterance training and test partitions provide the same assessment targets on different recordings \cite{speechocean,gopt}. The scoring model is trained on human ratings and evaluated against ratings of held-out recordings. Kim et al. adapt wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT with CTC, then combine contextual representations and reference text in a bidirectional LSTM scoring model, evaluating against expert scores on separate partitions of Korean learner speech and SpeechOcean762 \cite{kim}. Cai et al. connect hierarchical neural assessment to applied-linguistics constructs, adapting scoring toward human judgments of segmental, suprasegmental, and temporal performance \cite{cai}. Saito et al. use regression over phonological, prosodic, and temporal features to predict comprehensibility, testing generalization on additional recordings and an interview task \cite{saito}. These studies train scoring models against human ratings and assess multiple dimensions or transfer to new data, although differences in targets, rating panels, and metrics prevent direct numerical comparison with \textit{oollim}. XLS-R extends cross-lingual self-supervised speech representation learning \cite{xlsr}; XEUS learns multilingual representations across thousands of languages \cite{xeus}; PhoneticXeus adds phone recognition and supplies hidden representations and phone evidence \cite{phonetic}. In \textit{oollim} their pretrained weights remain unchanged, while ridge regression learns regularized linear mappings to assessment labels \cite{ridge}. \textit{oollim} combines these representations with reference-phone diagnostics and construct-specific regressions trained on one or more corpora, then evaluates them on an independently collected passage-reading cohort to test transfer beyond holding out recordings within a training corpus. Earlier neural-head, temporal, and acoustic-feature experiments motivated the ridge baseline; the evaluation does not cover every neural assessment architecture. The comparison therefore focuses on the external transfer of compact scoring models.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Audio, reference text, and representations}
Whole-recording embeddings and phone diagnostics derived from required reference text provide the scoring inputs. FFmpeg \cite{ffmpeg} decodes audio-bearing containers; a common preprocessing path averages channels to mono and resamples to 16 kHz. The full recording is processed as one utterance, without averaging separately scored sentences. Each encoder adapter returns frame embeddings and valid-frame lengths or masks, with XLS-R 300M, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus each providing 1,024-dimensional frame representations in the configurations used here \cite{xlsr,xeus,phonetic}. Statistics pooling \cite{pooling}, using masked temporal means and population standard deviations, with $10^{-5}$ added to the variance before the square root, yields 2,048-dimensional utterance vectors. Model revisions and implementation checksums are pinned in the repository. A pinned CMU pronunciation dictionary supplies ARPAbet reference pronunciations \cite{cmudict}. ARPAbet--IPA correspondences are documented in CMU dictionary tools \cite{cmutools}; the versioned mapping in \textit{oollim} converts dictionary phones to the PhoneticXeus token inventory, including explicit diphthong splitting and unstressed-vowel handling. PhoneticXeus provides frame-level phone probabilities and a greedy connectionist temporal classification (CTC) sequence \cite{phonetic,ctc}. Seventeen diagnostics summarize phone probabilities and entropy \cite{shannon}, blank behavior, predicted and expected phone rates, reference coverage, relative sequence length, edit operations \cite{editdistance}, and CTC negative log-likelihoods normalized by frame and reference-token counts \cite{ctc}. The CTC calculation sums valid paths for the expected phone sequence rather than assigning a single forced alignment \cite{ctc}. These features combine pronunciation, timing, and uncertainty evidence; they are not error-free phonetic diagnoses. Out-of-vocabulary references are rejected explicitly. The evaluated accuracy configuration uses XEUS + PhoneticXeus + IPA diagnostics, whereas fluency and prosody use XLS-R + XEUS. The scoring pipeline can select a representation combination and training family separately for each construct.

\subsection{Training corpora and label mappings}
Three research corpora supply assessment labels mapped to a common 0--10 range. SpeechOcean762 supplies utterance accuracy, fluency, and prosodic ratings on the 0--10 scale. The official SpeechOcean762 training partition is divided by speaker into 2,000 training and 500 development utterances; the official test partition is excluded. The Korean Learners' L2 Speech Corpus (English) \cite{han} is distributed as AIHub dataset 71463 \cite{aihub}. Eligible Training PRN recordings yield 2,699 training and 636 development examples. The corpus rubric rates pronunciation accuracy (segment clarity and errors) and prosodic fluency (stress, rhythm, intonation, rate, and pauses) on 1--5 scales \cite{han}. The supplied articulation score trains accuracy, while the prosodic-fluency score trains both fluency and prosody after mapping with $2.5(y-1)$. J-AESOP \cite{aesop} supplies 180 Japanese L2 speakers reading the North Wind and Sun passage (task 6\_01). Deterministic speaker partitions allocate 108 recordings to training, 36 to development, and 36 to a reserved partition; native controls are excluded. Ratings from 16 raters across three sections are averaged per construct. Segment ratings supply accuracy, alongside fluency and prosody; $10(y-1)/9$ maps the 1--10 source scale to 0--10. Partitions are speaker-disjoint within each corpus to separate development from fitting \cite{cawley}. In the Korean Learners' L2 Speech Corpus (English), recorder identities linked by identical decoded audio are grouped before splitting; duration and dictionary-coverage checks determine eligibility. Exact sampling rules, audio audits, mappings, and development results are retained in the reproduction package. Equal numerical ranges do not establish score equivalence \cite{dorans}; the shared prosody/fluency target does not provide an independent fluency annotation. The mappings permit joint fitting while retaining these differences in supervision.

\subsection{Ridge fitting and family selection}
Seven training families compare single-corpus and mixed-corpus supervision. The single-corpus families are \texttt{speechocean}, \texttt{korean}, and \texttt{aesop}; \texttt{korean} abbreviates the Korean Learners' L2 Speech Corpus (English) in model identifiers. The pairs are \texttt{koreanocean}, \texttt{aesopocean}, and \texttt{koreanaesop}, while the three-corpus family is \texttt{aesopkoreanocean}. In the matched family procedure, a recording from corpus $c$ receives weight $w_i=1/(K n_c)$, where $K$ is the number of included corpora and $n_c$ their respective training counts. Training-only weighted means and standard deviations standardize features \cite{esl}; a block of width $d_b$ receives gain $\sqrt{2048/d_b}$. Coordinates with standard deviation below $10^{-8}$ use scale 1. Each construct fits a weighted ridge objective \cite{ridge,esl}
\begin{equation}
\min_{\beta,b}\quad\sum_i w_i(y_i-z_i^\top\beta-b)^2+\alpha\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2,
\end{equation}
with an unpenalized intercept. Penalties $\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1,10,100\}$ are compared using mean development Spearman correlation over the included corpora; ties prefer lower mean MAE and then larger regularization, keeping selection on development data \cite{cawley}. Multi-output array storage does not couple the separate squared-loss objectives. All seven families are evaluated under matched construct-specific development-Spearman selection, including a fresh SpeechOcean refit; the main table reports construct-specific leaders across all three selection conditions. Two sensitivity conditions select penalties by development accuracy MAE: one uses the same grid, and the other uses the historical grid converted to normalized loss. Their full comparison and the original historical SpeechOcean results are retained in the supplementary materials and aggregate release. Public-development selection retained koreanocean for accuracy and aesopkoreanocean for fluency and prosody; these ranking-oriented research selections are distinct from the column maxima in the external table and are not claimed to maximize external QWK. The representation sensitivity analysis is separate from the fixed-combination external evaluation. A sensitivity check of all 315 family--construct--representation configurations on public development data retained the overall construct-specific selections under the 0.01 Spearman improvement threshold and corpus-level degradation and uncertainty checks recorded before fitting in the October 18, 2026 ablation protocol; the full protocol and results are provided in the reproduction materials. All coefficient estimation, preprocessing fitting, and score calibration exclude private evaluation ratings.

\subsection{Local execution and capacity}
Local operation was verified on consumer hardware using CPU inference. On the Windows gaming PC (Intel Core i7-11700K, 32 GB physical RAM, Ubuntu under WSL2 with approximately 24 GiB available RAM and 8 GiB swap), an archived 120-second capacity test completed in approximately 169 seconds, reaching 22.52 GiB peak process resident memory and 5.51 GiB peak system-wide swap use. The installed RTX 3080 was not required for that inference test. A separate Apple Silicon installation test processed an 8.21-second public recording in 42.13 seconds with four CPU threads, including decoding, all three encoders, phone features, and scoring. Software versions for the same WSL installation are recorded in the appendices. These single-run checks establish operation rather than representative throughput; the released duration limit is 120 seconds, and long recordings can require substantial RAM and swap.

\section{External evaluation}
\subsection{Independent recordings and ratings}
The collection comprised university EFL learners from China ($n=98$), Korea ($n=101$), and Japan ($n=107$), who recorded the same ``Please Call Stella'' passage on personal smartphones. One recording was excluded because of its length, leaving 305 samples. The three university professors had approximately 20--30 years of teaching experience, including pronunciation instruction, Cambridge English speaking assessment, and high-stakes speech rating. Based in those countries, the raters calibrated their judgments on separate recordings spanning proficiency levels, discussing discrepancies to establish shared criteria for accuracy, fluency, and prosody. Each professor then rated every recording independently in randomized order, blinded to participant identity and automated scores, using a 0--100 scale in five-point increments. The private evaluation recordings and ratings were held out from model training, hyperparameter tuning, and score calibration. The original human ratings and Azure API scores are archival; the seven ridge families were evaluated for the present study against those existing ratings. Azure scores were obtained in January 2026 using the \texttt{en-US} locale, with the Stella reference text supplied and each complete recording assessed as a whole. The returned accuracy, fluency, and prosody scores were retained. The service revision and remaining request options were not retained in the supplied evaluation archive; the comparison therefore concerns those recorded API outputs rather than a newly standardized Azure benchmark.

\subsection{Metrics and human baselines}
Evaluation distinguishes rank consistency from numerical agreement \cite{stemler,williamson}. Spearman's $\rho$ measures rank association \cite{spearman}. Quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) measures chance-corrected agreement with squared category-distance penalties \cite{cohen}; this study uses fixed integer categories 0--100 and half-up rounding. Model outputs are multiplied by ten; no calibration is fitted. Predictions are clipped to 0--100 for QWK, including bootstrap replicates, whereas Spearman retains raw predictions. A reference mean requires all three human scores; partial means are not substituted. Human--Human pairwise agreement averages the three pairwise statistics, whereas Human--Human consensus agreement averages each rater's agreement with the mean of the other two, never including that rater in their own reference. For each system, the three-rater row compares the system's scores with the mean of all humans, while the two-rater row averages the three separate agreement statistics against the three possible two-human means. Statistics are averaged, not observations or reference means pooled. The two-rater system row and Human--Human consensus row therefore provide the appropriate reference-size comparison when recomputed on the same recordings. Percentile 95\% intervals resample speaker clusters jointly, recomputing all component statistics within each replicate \cite{efron,field}. The supplementary marginal intervals retain the supplied evaluation export (2,000 replicates, seed 42). Additional paired comparisons use 200,000 bootstrap replicates (seed 42), subtracting statistics within each replicate on the same sampled recordings. An independent run with seed 2026 agrees on 89 of 90 adjusted interval decisions; matched SpeechOcean prosody correlation versus Azure is borderline. Each unique recording URL is treated as one speaker. Both percentile 95\% intervals and Bonferroni-adjusted intervals across the 90 matched-Spearman differences are supplied: eight systems versus human consensus and seven ridges versus Azure, for three constructs and two metrics. These post-evaluation comparisons condition on the observed raters and fitted models; adjustment covers the reported contrasts, not the preceding model search. The appendices report all reference comparisons and intervals for the same 305 recordings. Matched reference sizes and cohorts make the human and model estimates comparable.

\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:external} reports the leading configurations and all near-ties under the .001 display threshold. Rank association and numerical agreement favor different scorers: aesopocean leads accuracy correlation, whereas koreanaesop and aesopkoreanocean supply the fluency correlation leaders and koreanaesop supplies all prosody correlation leaders. Koreanocean leads QWK across constructs, using S for accuracy and H for fluency and prosody. Accuracy QWK exceeds the human baseline; fluency QWK is approximately equal to that baseline, while prosody remains below. All displayed models exceed Azure on both metrics. These maxima describe the completed external comparison rather than a prospectively locked choice. Separate paired analyses of the matched S subset retain positive Bonferroni-adjusted intervals in both seeds for 20 of 21 ridge--Azure correlation differences and for koreanocean accuracy QWK versus human consensus; matched SpeechOcean prosody correlation versus Azure is borderline. Those analyses do not establish superiority for the displayed D or H configurations. The supplement preserves all configurations, intervals, and paired contrasts.


\section{Discussion}
The results favor selecting scorers by construct and intended use. Public-development selection prioritizes rank correlation; users seeking numerical feedback should also consider QWK, for which the external table favors koreanocean with construct-specific selection rules. Those external preferences remain descriptive rather than independently validated deployment choices. Azure scores average 23.63, 27.88, and 10.57 points above the three-human means for accuracy, fluency, and prosody, respectively; these offsets help explain low QWK despite positive rank associations. No correction was fitted to remove those offsets. XEUS alone approaches the retained combinations on public-development macro correlation (gaps of 0.0017 for accuracy, 0.0016 for fluency, and 0.0041 for prosody in the selected families), so the sweep does not establish a substantial benefit from every additional encoder. A lighter XEUS-only system warrants separate external evaluation; the present private results apply to the evaluated combinations. Korean-only fluency and prosody share features and targets, so their predictions do not constitute independent learning of two constructs. On the same reference pairs and recordings, leading model point estimates exceed the Human--Human consensus baseline for rank association across constructs and for accuracy QWK, while the leading fluency QWK is only slightly higher and prosody QWK remains lower. The advantage over human agreement therefore depends on the construct and metric, with paired uncertainty for the matched-Spearman configurations reported in the appendices. External family rankings are descriptive rather than prospectively validated selections \cite{cawley}. Agreement with human ratings supports a particular score interpretation, rather than every educational use of the scores \cite{kane}. The software, trained scoring weights, model configurations, and reproduction materials are available at \url{https://github.com/cburst/oollim}, subject to the licenses documented in the repository. Practical scorer selection should therefore consider the target construct, agreement metric, and teaching context.

\section{Limitations}
The evaluation supports conclusions about one passage and three university EFL settings. Recording-level scores permit paired analysis, but URL-based grouping assumes one recording per speaker. The cohort excludes one long recording, and the 120-second operational limit constrains input coverage. Score-scale mappings do not establish equivalent constructs: the Korean Learners' L2 Speech Corpus (English) supplies shared prosody/fluency supervision, so differences among training families cannot be attributed solely to additional data. Speaker-disjoint fitting also cannot resolve possible upstream exposure of PhoneticXeus to learner corpora. The Azure service revision and remaining request options are unavailable, limiting exact replication of that comparator. The public-development ablation used a recorded selection rule, but neither bootstrap intervals nor contrast adjustment account for the entire model-search process. Broader claims require additional passages, populations, and intended uses \cite{kane}.

\section{Conclusion}
XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus, combined with construct-specific ridge scoring, support local evaluation of L2 English accuracy, fluency, and prosody on the tested consumer configuration. The external passage evaluation shows strong rank association with human ratings, with every ridge family exceeding correlations from the archived January 2026 Azure API outputs across all three constructs. Numerical agreement varies by construct and training family and identifies different leading scorers from rank association, supporting the reporting of both metrics for each construct. Additional passages and populations are needed to establish the scope of comparative human-agreement claims.

\section{Selection-rule sensitivity comparison}\label{sec:selectionrules}
Three public-development selection conditions were fixed before this comparison: S selects each construct by Spearman on $\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1,10,100\}$; D selects by development accuracy MAE on the same grid; H selects by accuracy MAE on $\{0.00005,0.0005,0.005,0.05,0.5,5\}$. The historical summed-loss penalties $\{0.1,1,10,100,1000,10000\}$ were divided by the original 2,000 SpeechOcean training utterances to express the same penalty strength under normalized loss. That fixed conversion is applied to every family, not divided again by its sample count. Corpus-balanced weights, training-only scaling, feature combinations, and partitions are identical across conditions. Development metrics are averaged over the included corpora. S breaks ties by lower MAE, then larger penalty; D and H break ties by larger penalty. Accuracy MAE selects one penalty per representation combination, so fluency and prosody share the penalty selected for their common XLS-R + XEUS features; their output coefficients remain separate. H reproduces the historical grid within the matched fitting convention, not every historical implementation detail.
All 21 family--condition combinations completed all 305 recordings. The main table displays the external leaders across S, D, and H, including every configuration within .001 of the highest unrounded correlation or QWK for each construct. The complete matrix below preserves the common-condition comparisons; the matched SpeechOcean refit replaces the historical deployment in S. No private ratings entered coefficient estimation, tuning, or calibration. Choosing a deployment from these external comparisons would be a post-evaluation choice. The separate historical deployed SpeechOcean scores remain in the released aggregate file and are not substituted for the matched S row.
Historical-grid DAMAE improves koreanocean two-rater fluency QWK from 0.585 to 0.744 and prosody QWK from 0.546 to 0.675, while the corresponding correlations change from 0.840 to 0.838 and 0.850 to 0.840. The fluency QWK point estimate is slightly above the human baseline of 0.739; no paired superiority test is reported for that sensitivity contrast. Different rules therefore trade rank association against numerical agreement, without establishing one uniformly best condition. The intervals below are marginal, not intervals for condition differences; the 90-contrast adjustment in the following section concerns the matched S subset only. The released CSV also includes every three-rater result and its interval.

\subsection{Korean scoring and target mapping}
Han, Chung, and Kim \cite{han} define two 1--5 ratings: pronunciation accuracy and prosodic fluency. Accuracy addresses segmental clarity and errors; prosodic fluency combines stress, rhythm, intonation, speed, and pausing. The stored fields are \texttt{articulationScore} and \texttt{prosodyScore}. The second field supplies both fluency and prosody targets in the present implementation. Mapping $2.5(y-1)$ produces 0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 from the five integer levels; mapping does not equate the rubric with SpeechOcean or create an independent fluency annotation. Korean-only heads use the same features and targets for both constructs, yielding identical predictions in the evaluated configuration.

\subsection{Ablation protocol}
The protocol was recorded before fitting on October 18, 2026 (repository: \nolinkurl{results/ablation-mac-v1/PROTOCOL.md}). The completed public-development sweep crossed seven training families, three constructs, and all 15 nonempty subsets of four feature blocks, giving 315 configurations and 945 corpus-level comparisons. X denotes XEUS, R XLS-R, P PhoneticXeus embeddings, and I the 17 phone diagnostics; I still requires PhoneticXeus inference, so omitting P does not necessarily avoid that encoder. Existing speaker-disjoint partitions, corpus-balanced weights, training-only standardization, and block gains were preserved. Each configuration selected a penalty from $\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1,10,100\}$ using equal-weight mean Spearman across all three development corpora, breaking ties by lower macro MAE and then larger penalty. This differs from the earlier included-corpus tuning; no corpus in these tables is an untouched cross-corpus test. Private recordings and ratings were excluded from fitting and selection.

Each candidate was compared with the fixed-feature baseline refitted under the same sweep protocol (X+P+I for accuracy, X+R otherwise). Candidates within 0.01 of the numerical best were shortlisted. Promotion required macro Spearman gain at least 0.01, no corpus correlation loss exceeding 0.02, no corpus MAE increase exceeding 0.10 on the 0--10 scale, and a positive lower 95\% paired speaker-bootstrap bound (2,000 draws, seed 42). Fewer blocks were preferred where benefits were inconclusive. These screening gates do not constitute correction for all search comparisons. Deployed heads were not automatically replaced.

The decision table distinguishes numerical maxima from candidates passing the complete gate. Two AESOP-only alternatives passed: X+P for fluency and X+P+I for prosody. Their macro gains over the refitted AESOP baselines were 0.0341 (95\% interval [0.0118, 0.0619]) for fluency and 0.0389 ([0.0161, 0.0638]) for prosody. Both exceed the 0.01 within-family threshold. Neither became the overall construct selection, and neither was substituted into the external seven-family evaluation. The overall retained choices were koreanocean X+P+I accuracy and aesopkoreanocean X+R fluency/prosody. Thus retaining the overall configurations does not mean that no individual family improved. \section{External rater backgrounds and procedure}
The original human-evaluation study reports three experienced university raters. The Korea-based rater had more than 20 years of teaching experience, including a decade of university pronunciation instruction and speaking assessment. The Japan-based rater had nearly 30 years of teaching experience and more than 10 years as a Cambridge English speaking assessor. The China-based rater had nearly 20 years of experience, including high-stakes speech rating and university speaking assessment in an international joint-degree program. All three held professorial positions, as confirmed by the study author.

Before evaluating the source recordings, the raters jointly assessed separate Stella recordings spanning proficiency levels and discussed discrepancies to establish shared criteria for segmental accuracy, fluency, and prosody. They then scored the 306 source recordings independently in randomized order, blinded to participant identity and automated scores. Ratings used a 0--100 scale in five-point increments, with communicative-effectiveness anchors labelled ``High Intelligibility'' above 85 and ``Low Intelligibility'' below 40. These are the documented general scale anchors, not a newly constructed analytic rubric for each dimension. The original study's authors served as its raters; their ratings were reused as the external reference for the present model evaluation. One recording was subsequently excluded for excessive duration, leaving the matched 305-recording cohort. Human calibration used separate recordings and did not fit or calibrate any automated scorer.
